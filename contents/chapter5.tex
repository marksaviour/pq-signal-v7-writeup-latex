%Chapter 5
\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This chapter reports the evidence generated by the procedures of Chapter~\ref{sec:methodology} and interprets it against the requirements of Chapter~\ref{sec:design}. It follows the reporting rule established there. A demonstrated finding is one the evidence directly supports. A conditional claim identifies the condition on which it depends. An unresolved issue is one the evidence does not settle. An unmet requirement is one that inspection shows the artefact does not satisfy. The scope of every performance claim is the post-quantum handshake and its transcript authentication. The Double Ratchet continuation that follows the handshake, together with the encrypted payload and the remainder of the session, retains the classical X25519 exchange and lies outside the scope defined in Section~\ref{subsubsec:req-functional}. Where a measurement includes the first encrypted message, the classical ratchet step it contains is identified as such and is not counted as a post-quantum cost.

Two machines were used, as specified in Section~\ref{subsec:environment}. The primary machine is the Zen~5 desktop; the secondary machine is the Apple M1~Pro. Absolute timings differ between them, and no value is averaged or otherwise combined across the two. The evidence for every comparison is the ordering observed within a single machine, repeated on the second machine as a replication. All timing figures are the median of five invocation-level medians, one median per measurement block; the bracketed interval gives the full range of those five medians. All serialised sizes are exact, produced once per configuration from the fixed inputs of Section~\ref{subsec:size-measurement}.

%Chapter 5.1
\subsection{Verification Outcomes and the Requirement-to-Evidence Matrix}
\label{subsec:verification-outcomes}

Before any timing measurement was accepted, each pinned artefact was subjected to the functional checks defined in Section~\ref{subsec:correctness}. The checks execute the two handshake modes, confirm that the responder derives the initiator's shared secret, and confirm that a tampered transcript signature and a tampered pre-key signature are both rejected. On the primary machine the baseline suite reported eighty-nine passing tests with none failing and four ignored; each fully post-quantum version reported sixty-eight passing tests with none failing and two ignored. The four ignored baseline tests and the two ignored version tests are inherited upstream cases that the upstream project marks as slow to run locally, together with one documentation example; none bears on the handshake. The vendored HQC-256 implementation passed its own known-answer and round-trip suite of six tests and one documentation test. The same suites were re-run on the secondary machine with identical outcomes, so the functional behaviour is reproduced on both architectures. These results are the precondition the reporting rule requires, and every performance figure in the remainder of this chapter is drawn from the same pinned trees.

The difference in test count between the baseline and the two versions is not a regression in the measured handshake. It follows from the removal of inherited regression tests that exercise behaviour outside the handshake scope, and from the Sealed Sender suite, which does not compile against a signing-only identity and is therefore absent under the version feature sets. The functional checks that bear on the handshake, agreement in both modes and rejection of both tampering cases, are present and passing on every version.

Table~\ref{tab:req-evidence} maps each requirement of Section~\ref{subsec:objectives} to the evidence that bears on it and records the verdict under the reporting rule. The functional and non-functional requirements are demonstrated. Several security requirements are demonstrated for the properties the design does establish, and the remainder are recorded as conditional or unmet, with the specific gap named. This matrix is the single place where the project's claims and its limitations are stated together; the subsections that follow supply the quantitative evidence it references, and Section~\ref{subsec:discussion} interprets it.

\subsection{Primitive Performance}
\label{subsec:primitive-results}

The primitive measurements isolate the cost of the individual cryptographic operations, independent of the handshake that combines them. They were produced from the common primitive-harness checkout described in Section~\ref{subsec:perf-measurement}, so a single build supplied every KEM. The operations measured are key generation, encapsulation, and decapsulation for each key-encapsulation mechanism, and key generation, signing, and verification for ML-DSA-87. The baseline's classical primitives, X25519 key generation and agreement and the XEdDSA signature operations, were measured from the baseline harness. Kyber-1024 and ML-KEM-1024 are the same lattice scheme: Kyber-1024 is the round-three revision that the baseline runs as its operative primitive, and ML-KEM-1024 is its standardised form as implemented for Version~1. Their public keys and ciphertexts are identically sized, so the size arithmetic of Section~\ref{subsec:size-results} is unaffected by the substitution, while their measured timings reflect the two different implementations.

The lattice and hash-and-sign primitives are fast, and the code-based primitive is not. On the primary machine ML-KEM-1024 key generation, encapsulation, and decapsulation measured 14.806~\textmu s, 11.874~\textmu s, and 12.726~\textmu s respectively; the round-three Kyber-1024 baseline measured 14.857~\textmu s, 14.797~\textmu s, and 17.837~\textmu s for the same three operations. ML-DSA-87 key generation, signing, and verification measured 36.102~\textmu s, 84.485~\textmu s, and 39.949~\textmu s. The classical baseline primitives were faster still: X25519 key generation and agreement measured 9.780~\textmu s and 26.570~\textmu s, and XEdDSA signing and verification measured 20.356~\textmu s and 18.089~\textmu s. Against these, HQC-256 measured 683.151~\textmu s for key generation, 1.359~ms for encapsulation, and 2.130~ms for decapsulation.

The gap between HQC-256 and ML-KEM-1024 is large and consistent. On the primary machine HQC-256 key generation cost approximately forty-six times ML-KEM-1024 key generation, encapsulation approximately one hundred and fourteen times, and decapsulation approximately one hundred and sixty-seven times. The secondary machine reproduced the same directions with ratios of approximately thirty-eight, one hundred and three, and two hundred and sixteen. Across all five blocks on both machines the direction never reversed and the invocation-median ranges did not overlap, so the ordering of HQC-256 above ML-KEM-1024 is stable in the sense defined in Section~\ref{subsec:perf-measurement}. The comparison between ML-KEM-1024 and round-three Kyber-1024 key generation does not meet that standard: the direction changes between machines and the ranges overlap, which is the expected result for two implementations of the same operation whose true costs are close.

\subsection{Handshake Performance}
\label{subsec:handshake-results}

The handshake measurements time the two bounded spans defined in Section~\ref{subsec:perf-measurement}. The initiator span runs from the point at which the initiator begins processing the responder's bundle to the serialisation of the initial message. The responder span runs from the serialised initial message, with a fresh store, to successful derivation of the session. Each span is measured in both pre-key modes: the last-resort-only mode, in which the responder publishes a signed last-resort KEM pre-key, and the full mode, which adds a signed one-time KEM pre-key. Store construction and cloning lie outside the timed region in every case.

The handshake results track the primitive results, as they must, because the handshake cost is dominated by the KEM operations it performs. Table~\ref{tab:handshake} reports the spans in both modes on both machines, together with the integrated exchange for context. On the primary machine the baseline initiator span measured 213.549~\textmu s and the baseline responder span 195.574~\textmu s. Version~1 measured 321.802~\textmu s and 157.495~\textmu s for the same two spans; its initiator is slower than the baseline, because it performs an ML-KEM encapsulation and an ML-DSA transcript signature where the baseline performs a Kyber encapsulation and an XEdDSA signature, while its responder betters the baseline on this machine and is at parity with it on the secondary. Version~2 measured 3.072~ms and 4.425~ms, between fourteen and twenty-three times the baseline, the increase attributable to the HQC-256 operations the handshake now performs.

The comparison of principal interest, Version~2 against Version~1, is stable throughout. On the primary machine the Version~2 initiator span was 9.55 times the Version~1 initiator span, the responder span 28.09 times, and the integrated exchange of Section~\ref{subsec:perf-measurement} 12.77 times. On the secondary machine the same three ratios were 17.04, 62.51, and 26.29. The directions are identical on both machines and the invocation-median ranges do not overlap, so Version~2 is slower than Version~1 across every measured span in the stable sense. The last-resort-only spans, which perform one fewer encapsulation, are correspondingly faster than the full-mode spans on both versions and both machines, but do not change any ordering: on the primary machine the Version~2 initiator span fell from 3.072~ms in full mode to 1.652~ms in last-resort-only mode, and the Version~1 initiator span from 321.802~\textmu s to 264.687~\textmu s, while Version~2 remained far above Version~1 in both modes.

\subsection{Size and Storage Results}
\label{subsec:size-results}

The size measurements are exact and deterministic, captured once per configuration from the fixed inputs of Section~\ref{subsec:size-measurement}, and were byte-for-byte identical on both machines. They record two quantities: the serialised cryptographic components of the published pre-key bundle, and the serialised protocol objects exchanged, namely the initial \texttt{PreKeySignalMessage} and the first reply. Because the primitives are unchanged across machines, these figures are properties of the pinned serialisers and the chosen primitives alone.

The size cost of the post-quantum transition is far larger than its time cost, and it does not amortise. Table~\ref{tab:sizes} reports the figures. In full mode the initial message measured 1{,}754~bytes for the baseline, 10{,}517~bytes for Version~1, and 36{,}223~bytes for Version~2, approximately six times and twenty-one times the baseline respectively. The first reply measured 98~bytes for all three artefacts and in both modes, because it is an ordinary Double Ratchet message that carries no KEM material. This is the central asymmetry of the results: the handshake's computational cost is paid once and fades across a long session, while the initial message's size, dominated by the 14{,}422-byte HQC-256 ciphertext in Version~2, is paid on every fresh session and the corresponding pre-key storage is paid for every published bundle. The baseline's optional mode varies a one-time X25519 pre-key, a difference of three bytes, whereas the versions' optional mode varies a full one-time KEM pre-key; the two optional modes are therefore not equivalent and the tables label them accordingly.

Server-side storage is not measured directly. It is derived from the component sizes above, as established in Section~\ref{subsec:size-measurement}: each published bundle occupies its serialised cryptographic component total, and a server holding one bundle per user multiplies that figure by the user population. The component totals in Table~\ref{tab:sizes} are therefore the per-user storage figures, and the storage cost of Version~2 relative to the baseline is the ratio of those totals.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{subsec:discussion}

The results answer the empirical question this project set. Within the handshake scope, a fully post-quantum PQXDH built on ML-KEM-1024 is inexpensive: Version~1 costs 1.23 times the deployed baseline end to end on the primary machine and 1.62 times on the secondary, its responder span betters the baseline on the primary machine and matches it on the secondary, and its message grows by roughly a factor of six, from under two kilobytes to about ten. A fully post-quantum PQXDH built on HQC-256 is not inexpensive on either axis: its handshake costs between roughly ten and sixty times Version~1 depending on the span and the machine, and its initial message is about thirty-six kilobytes, more than twenty times the baseline. Both instantiations produce a working handshake; they differ by which cost the choice of KEM imposes.

Two features of the evidence bound these conclusions. First, the ordering claims rest on the stability criterion of Section~\ref{subsec:perf-measurement}, non-overlapping invocation-median ranges with a consistent direction across five blocks on each machine, and they are reported only where that criterion is met. The HQC-256-above-ML-KEM-1024 ordering and the Version~2-above-Version~1 ordering meet it on both machines; the ML-KEM-1024-against-Kyber-1024 comparison does not, and is reported as unresolved rather than forced into a direction. Second, the measurement isolates the handshake. The spans exclude store preparation, and the one measurement that includes the first encrypted message, the integrated exchange, contains the classical X25519 ratchet step and is read as context for the handshake spans rather than as a second independent comparison. The size figures, being exact, carry no such qualification.

The results are also bounded by what the artefact is. The performance figures characterise one pinned pre-standardisation implementation of each primitive on desktop-class hardware, not the algorithms in general and not constrained or mobile devices; the HQC-256 figures in particular measure a pure-Rust implementation that predates any standard. The security verdicts of Table~\ref{tab:req-evidence} stand: the handshake establishes a post-quantum shared secret and authenticates its transcript, but ciphertext binding is signature-mediated rather than bound into the key derivation, suite enforcement is not present at the constructors, and the binding property PQXDH requires of its KEM is not established for the pinned HQC revision. These are the reasons the artefact is an experimental research prototype rather than a deployment candidate, and they set the agenda that Chapter~\ref{sec:lsepi} and Chapter~\ref{sec:conclusion} take up.
