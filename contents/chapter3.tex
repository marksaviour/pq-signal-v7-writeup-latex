%Chapter 3
\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}
Using the published evidence, the literature survey in Chapter~\ref{sec:lit} narrowed the field to ML-KEM-1024 and HQC as the candidate KEMs, and ML-DSA-87 as the candidate signature scheme, leaving the final formal selection to this chapter.

A defensible selection requires concrete functional, security, and non-functional requirements; a threat model against which the security claims can be assessed; and criteria that reflect Signal's deployment context.

These elements support the formal selection of the two KEMs and the signature scheme. Two design choices follow: whether to retain a classical key-agreement component alongside the post-quantum primitives, and how to authenticate both parties when the identity key is no longer used for Diffie--Hellman.

%Chapter 3.1
\subsection{Objectives and Requirements}
\label{subsec:objectives}

The project's aim and research questions express its objectives in conceptual terms. This section translates them into concrete requirements against which the artefact and its evaluation can be assessed.

%Chapter 3.1.1
\subsubsection{Functional Requirements}
\label{subsubsec:req-functional}

The artefact comprises two separately maintained PQXDH-derived asynchronous handshakes: Version 1 uses ML-KEM-1024 and ML-DSA-87, and Version 2 substitutes HQC-256 within the same structure. Both preserve the one-message pre-key workflow and HKDF derivation but remove X25519 from the bootstrap KDF; the unchanged Double Ratchet nevertheless mixes X25519 into the chain that protects the first payload.

Here, a ``fully post-quantum handshake'' means only the KEM bootstrap and ML-DSA transcript authentication, not the Double Ratchet, encrypted payload, or complete session. Changed message processing makes the variants non-interoperable with deployed PQXDH and invalidates transfer of its existing formal analysis.

The initiator verifies signatures on the responder's ratchet, last-resort, and optional one-time keys, encapsulates to the selected KEM keys, signs the canonical transcript, and derives bootstrap root and chain material from the KEM secrets through HKDF. The responder decapsulates, derives the same material, and verifies the initiator before accepting.

Each version must complete bundle generation, signature verification, encapsulation, decapsulation, transcript authentication, and derivation. It must bind identities, suite, key roles, and identifiers to canonical encodings, authenticate the initial encrypted message before acceptance, and erase transient secrets and consumed one-time private keys. Detailed acceptance criteria are in Appendix~\ref{app:extended-requirements}.

The implemented modes are last-resort-only and last-resort-plus-one-time: unlike deployed PQXDH's either/or choice, each variant always uses the last-resort KEM key and optionally adds a one-time KEM key. Appendix~\ref{app:extended-requirements} gives the exact semantics.

Automated tests must demonstrate both modes and rejection after independently altering signatures, signed keys, identifiers, ciphertexts, transcript fields, or the encrypted first message, and must reject malformed or cross-version encodings and exact replays. Any delegated replay-protection mechanism must be identified. Appendix~\ref{app:extended-requirements} gives the detailed criteria.

%Chapter 3.1.2
\subsubsection{Security Requirements}
\label{subsubsec:req-security}

The security claims in this subsection are limited to the PQXDH-derived KEM bootstrap and its transcript authentication. They do not apply once the Double Ratchet mixes X25519 into the chain used for the first encrypted payload, and they do not claim post-quantum security for the full messaging session.

Given the threat model of Section~\ref{subsec:threat-model} and an authenticated binding between each user and their ML-DSA-87 identity verification key, each artefact version must achieve mutual authentication against an active quantum-capable adversary: the responder through signatures on published pre-keys, the initiator through a signature on the handshake transcript. This is a stronger objective than deployed PQXDH's, whose Diffie--Hellman-based authentication does not resist active quantum adversaries, so it requires a separate argument rather than relying on PQXDH's existing analysis.

A completed handshake must remain confidential against a quantum-capable adversary unless the post-quantum decapsulation material for that session is compromised. Neither artefact version incorporates an X25519 agreement value into the handshake secret, so both lack the hybrid fallback of deployed PQXDH if the selected KEM is subsequently broken: confidentiality of the bootstrap secret rests solely on the selected KEM, and authentication on ML-DSA-87, under the stated assumptions about the KDF, randomness, transcript processing, and key erasure.

Forward secrecy is mode-dependent. Last-resort-only runs remain exposed to later compromise of the retained decapsulation key. In last-resort-plus-one-time mode, later compromise of the retained key alone must not reveal the run, provided that the additional key is authenticated as one-time and its private key and transient secret are erased after successful use. Later identity-signing-key compromise alone must not reveal an earlier handshake secret, although it may enable later impersonation. Appendix~\ref{app:extended-requirements} gives the full conditions.

Five further acceptance targets are not inherited from PQXDH. Session independence requires fresh randomness and prevents compromise of one run from aiding another. Key-compromise impersonation resistance prevents a compromised party's signing key from enabling impersonation of a different party. Identity-misbinding resistance commits both endpoints to the same identities and key material. Exact replay must not create a second session or duplicate plaintext, and a one-time pre-key must be accepted at most once. Appendix~\ref{app:extended-requirements} defines the detailed conditions.

Randomness must come from a cryptographically secure source, and transient secrets and consumed one-time keys must be erased. A malicious server may force the weaker last-resort-only mode by withholding one-time keys. Appendix~\ref{app:extended-requirements} gives the operational criteria.

The primitive constraints, IND-CCA security and shared-secret binding for the KEM and post-quantum EUF-CMA security for the signature, pass into Section~\ref{subsec:selection-framework}. Appendix~\ref{app:extended-requirements} records the detailed functional and security criteria, surrounding assumptions, and role-level signing specification.

Cryptographic deniability is not required for either artefact version because this project prioritises explicit post-quantum authentication. The initiator signs the handshake transcript with ML-DSA-87, allowing the responder to verify who started the session.

The claims assume that HKDF is secure on the specified inputs, that key encodings are pairwise disjoint, that all public inputs are validated, that randomness is cryptographically secure, and that expired material can be reliably erased. They also assume that each endpoint already has an authenticated copy of its peer's identity key and that the first-message encryption supplies quantum-resistant confidentiality and ciphertext integrity. The artefact instead inherits AES-256-CBC encrypt-then-MAC with an eight-byte HMAC-SHA-256 tag, so whether its 64-bit integrity bound meets the abstract post-quantum assumption remains unresolved. Appendix~\ref{app:extended-requirements} states the assumptions in full.

%Chapter 3.1.3
\subsubsection{Non-Functional Requirements}
\label{subsubsec:req-nonfunctional}

Three non-functional requirements shape both primitive selection and the evaluation. The first is security-category equivalence: every substituted primitive must target NIST security category~5, matching ML-KEM-1024 and the Kyber-1024 parameter set used by the baseline \cite{national_institute_of_standards_and_technology_us_module-lattice-based_2024-1,alagic_status_2022}. Holding the category constant prevents either source-code version from appearing cheaper simply because it offers a lower target security level.

The second requirement is fair comparison. Versions 1 and 2 retain the same handshake, authentication, fields, and procedure while changing the KEM suite. The baseline is pinned to the pre-SPQR libsignal v0.73.3 PQXDH path, which uses Kyber-1024; it represents that reproducible source configuration rather than every production client \cite{kret_pqxdh_nodate,noauthor_release_nodate}.

The third requirement is measurable and reproducible computation, primitive operations, serialised initial and reply objects, bundle components, and server-held cryptographic payload as a function of one-time-key inventory. Chapter~\ref{sec:methodology} fixes the procedure and exclusions. ``Handshake latency'' means bounded endpoint computation, not network or application delay.

%Chapter 3.2
\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{subsec:threat-model}

The requirements in Section~\ref{subsec:objectives} are assessed against five adversary models for the PQXDH-derived handshake. Their capabilities are considered separately unless a model or security claim explicitly combines them.

The first model is a classical active network adversary with complete message control but no endpoint compromise. The second is a passive HNDL adversary that records bundles and initial messages for later quantum attack. The third combines quantum computation during the run with active network control; X25519 then supplies neither confidentiality nor authentication, so the variants target stronger mutual authentication than deployed PQXDH.

The fourth model considers compromise of identity-signing, last-resort, one-time, and session keys separately and accounts for timing. An identity key obtained before or during a run enables later impersonation but does not itself reveal earlier completed secrets. Appendix~\ref{app:extended-requirements} states the remaining cases.

The fifth model is a malicious pre-key server that may withhold keys, return stale bundles, refuse service, or substitute unsigned metadata. It cannot forge ML-DSA-87 signatures, but the implemented signatures omit key roles and identifiers, so a valid KEM key can be moved between bundle fields unless the application authenticates that metadata.

Two assumptions bound all five models. Each party already has an authenticated copy of the other's identity verification key; a key delivered with its own signature cannot establish identity. Endpoints remain uncompromised except in explicit cases, randomness behaves correctly, and physical and timing side channels are outside scope.

%Chapter 3.3
\subsection{The Primitive Selection Framework}
\label{subsec:selection-framework}

The literature survey carried forward two KEMs and three signature schemes. Each is assessed against six criteria, three of which act as gates. First, its selected parameter set must target NIST security category~5, matching the pinned baseline in Section~\ref{subsubsec:req-nonfunctional}. Second, it must provide the basic security notion required of its primitive class: IND-CCA security for a KEM or post-quantum EUF-CMA security for a signature scheme. Third, it must have a Rust implementation that can be integrated into libsignal, because a candidate that cannot be built into the artefact cannot answer the empirical research questions.

KEMs face one further protocol-specific gate. The shared secret must satisfy the binding property required by a PQXDH-style handshake, or the protocol must add and validate an explicit mitigation. This requirement is separate from IND-CCA security. A KEM whose binding status remains unresolved can still serve as an experimental comparator, but it cannot support a claim that the resulting handshake is ready for deployment.

Three criteria rank candidates that pass the gates. Formal evidence covers proofs, scrutiny, and known gaps. Implementation maturity covers release status, vectors, and assurance, while cost covers size, performance, and integration. Maturity outranks a small speed advantage because an immature benchmark may measure the library rather than the algorithm.

%Chapter 3.4
\subsection{Applying the Framework}
\label{subsec:selection}

The framework is applied first to the KEM candidates, then to the signature candidates, and finally to the additional binding requirement that the handshake places on the KEM integration. This ordering separates the security of each primitive in isolation from the security of using that primitive inside the handshake.

%Chapter 3.4.1
\subsubsection{KEM Selection}
\label{subsubsec:kem-selection}

ML-KEM-1024 passes the gates: FIPS 203 fixes its category-5 encapsulation key and ciphertext at 1568 bytes, and Version 1 uses an integrable Rust implementation developed with verification tooling \cite{national_institute_of_standards_and_technology_us_module-lattice-based_2024-1,noauthor_release_nodate-1}. Published analysis also establishes the binding property required by PQXDH.

HQC-256 provides the code-based alternative. NIST selected HQC on 11 March 2025 because its quasi-cyclic decoding assumption offers a hedge against a future break in structured lattices \cite{alagic_status_2025}.

The absence of a standard makes the exact revision important: Version 2 vendors the unreleased RustCrypto \texttt{hqc-kem} package, version 0.1.0, at commit \texttt{8f1fd8c}, together with a one-line correction to deserialisation \cite{noauthor_kemshqc-kem_nodate}, implementing the 22 August 2025 HQC v5.0.0 specification and its salted Fujisaki--Okamoto transform \cite{aguilar-melchor_hqc_nodate}.

The v5.0.0 specification calls its category-5 instance HQC-5; this dissertation retains the implementation's HQC-256 name. Its expanded encapsulation key is 7237 bytes and its ciphertext 14421 bytes \cite{aguilar-melchor_hqc_nodate}. Chapter~\ref{sec:methodology} records the less mature pinned implementation and correction that bound the timing claims.

ML-KEM-1024 is selected as the primary KEM and HQC-256 as the experimental comparator. Baseline-to-Version-1 measures the aggregate fully post-quantum conversion rather than one primitive; Version-1-to-Version-2 principally changes the KEM but still includes implementation-quality differences.

%Chapter 3.4.2
\subsubsection{Signature Selection}
\label{subsubsec:sig-selection}

Chapter~\ref{sec:lit}'s comparison leaves ML-DSA-87 as the only candidate combining a final standard, moderate size, and an integrable Rust implementation, pinned here as \texttt{libcrux-ml-dsa} 0.0.8 \cite{noauthor_cratesio_2026}. Its signatures remain far larger than XEdDSA's, but the alternatives either multiply that bandwidth further or lack a final standard. Signature size affects both principal costs in this protocol: each signed bundle key carries one signature, and every initial message carries the initiator's transcript signature. Using ML-DSA-87 in both source-code versions also keeps the KEM comparison independent of a simultaneous change in signature scheme.

%Chapter 3.4.3
\subsubsection{The Binding Requirement and HQC}
\label{subsubsec:binding-check}

IND-CCA secrecy does not establish which public-key and ciphertext context a shared secret belongs to. In PQXDH, insufficient binding can let two runs accept the same secret under different key contexts, so the relevant requirement is adversarial rather than merely the improbability of accidental collisions \cite{bhargavan_formal_2024}. Fiedler and G\"unther proved the required game-based notion for Kyber and ML-KEM, but another KEM does not inherit that result \cite{fiedler_security_2024}. Where native binding is absent or unresolved, the protocol must unambiguously bind encoded public keys and ciphertexts into both transcript authentication and KDF inputs; associated data alone is only the specification's minimum mitigation. Appendix~\ref{app:binding-detail} gives the mechanism-level analysis.

ML-KEM-1024 passes the primitive-level binding gate on the published PQXDH-specific result \cite{fiedler_security_2024}; Appendix~\ref{app:binding-detail} records the supporting mechanism.

HQC needs a revision-specific verdict. Published work finds the earlier round-four construction deficient under several binding notions \cite{noauthor_binding_nodate}. Version 2 instead implements v5.0.0, whose successful shared-secret derivation incorporates a hash of the complete encapsulation key and a ciphertext-carried salt, then re-encrypts during decapsulation and uses implicit rejection on mismatch. Direct source inspection confirms that the pinned implementation follows this revised structure. Those changes provide relevant supporting evidence, but the published analysis does not prove that v5.0.0 satisfies the exact PQXDH-required notion. Version 2 therefore remains an experimental comparator. Appendix~\ref{app:binding-detail} gives the mechanism.

The protocol layer supplies only partial assurance. The transcript signature covers the KEM ciphertexts, identities, ratchet key, and base key, but omits KEM public keys and pre-key identifiers; the HKDF input contains neither keys nor ciphertexts. Thus the explicit wrapper described above remains unimplemented. ML-KEM retains its primitive-level result, while HQC remains an experimental comparator without the required proof.

%Chapter 3.5
\subsection{Design of the Fully Post-Quantum Handshake}
\label{subsec:variant-design}

The selected primitives become a complete handshake through three further decisions: whether to retain a classical agreement component, how to authenticate both parties once the identity key can no longer perform Diffie--Hellman, and how to encode, derive, and erase the resulting values.

%Chapter 3.5.1
\subsubsection{Hybrid versus Fully Post-Quantum}
\label{subsubsec:full-pq-decision}

Deployed migrations are hybrid, whereas these variants remove X25519 from the bootstrap KDF. This is an experimental control: a KEM-only bootstrap exposes the post-quantum agreement's cost and security consequences, while measurements containing the first payload still identify the common X25519 ratchet step.

The classical term cannot resist the stated quantum adversary; its value is fallback against a future KEM break. Removing it also exposes the authentication problem that a hybrid construction postpones: authenticating both parties without Diffie--Hellman.

Neither version retains PQXDH's hybrid fallback, so bootstrap confidentiality depends on the selected KEM. This is a research instrument, not a deployment recommendation; deployment would additionally require the measured evidence and resolution of the binding and metadata gaps.

%Chapter 3.5.2
\subsubsection{Authentication Design and the Deniability Trade}
\label{subsubsec:design-deniability}

Replacing the classical identity key with ML-DSA-87 makes authentication explicit in both directions. The responder signs the X25519 ratchet key, the last-resort KEM pre-key, and every one-time KEM pre-key under its long-term identity key, and the initiator verifies each signature before using the bundle; the initiator then signs the handshake transcript of Section~\ref{subsubsec:variant-spec}, which the responder verifies before accepting the session.

The implementation falls short: responder signatures omit role, suite, and numeric identifier, while the initiator transcript omits KEM public keys and identifiers. Extending the signatures and KDF input remains an obligation. A deniable post-quantum alternative such as RingXKEM would require a different handshake architecture and lacks a suitable complete Rust implementation, so it is outside scope \cite{katsumata_comprehensive_2025}.

This authentication design sacrifices qualified offline deniability. X3DH and PQXDH obtain that property because their transcript authentication derives from Diffie--Hellman values rather than from an initiator signature that a third party can verify \cite{kret_pqxdh_nodate}; an ML-DSA-87 transcript signature is transferable evidence that the corresponding identity key signed those bytes.

%Chapter 3.5.3
\subsubsection{Handshake Specification}
\label{subsubsec:variant-spec}

Both versions implement the same structure and differ semantically by KEM and domain-separation labels. A type-safety gap remains: constructors accept any supported KEM type although each version fixes its suite, so key and ciphertext types are not enforced at construction.

Appendix~\ref{app:handshake-spec} gives the field-level run. In outline, the initiator verifies the bundle, encapsulates to the last-resort and optional one-time keys, derives bootstrap material with suite-specific HKDF, and signs a length-prefixed transcript; the responder decapsulates, derives, and verifies before accepting. Pending retransmission reuses the same run. Figure~\ref{fig:pqxdh-derived-handshake-sequence} shows the flow.

    \centering

    \caption{High-level message flow of the PQXDH-derived handshake shared by both source-code versions.}
    \label{fig:pqxdh-derived-handshake-sequence}
\end{figure}

Key derivation must be domain-separated by mechanism rather than solely by intent: the algorithm-suite identifier must appear in both the signed transcript and the HKDF \texttt{info} input, and all encoded key types must occupy pairwise-disjoint encoding domains. The implemented labels satisfy the first condition by naming the KEM and signature suite in both the transcript label and the \texttt{info} value, while the one-byte algorithm identifiers give encoded keys disjoint domains. Domain separation complements transcript authentication and does not replace it.

The Double Ratchet then combines the KEM-derived root with an X25519 agreement before encrypting or decrypting the first payload, which is why the post-quantum claim of this chapter ends at the bootstrap and transcript-authentication boundary. Libsignal authenticates that encrypted message with an eight-byte HMAC-SHA-256 tag computed over the serialised message and both encoded identity keys, verified before AES-256-CBC decryption; the KEM ciphertext fields lie outside that encrypt-then-MAC input and rely instead on the transcript signature. Operational behaviour and secret-erasure evidence are in Appendix~\ref{app:spec-operational}.

%Chapter 3.7
\subsection{Chapter Summary}
\label{subsec:design-summary}

This chapter turned the findings of Chapter~\ref{sec:lit} into requirements, a threat model, and a selection framework. It selected ML-DSA-87 for authentication and ML-KEM-1024 as the primary KEM, retained HQC-256 as the experimental code-based comparator, and specified the two source-code versions, fixing the post-quantum claim at the bootstrap and transcript-authentication boundary. The obligations recorded along the way, role and identifier authentication, protocol-level binding, exact replay rejection, deletion of consumed one-time keys, and zeroisation, bound what the evaluation may claim: Chapter~\ref{sec:methodology} defines its measurements around these distinctions, and Chapter~\ref{sec:results} must report demonstrated, conditional, unresolved, and unmet results separately.
