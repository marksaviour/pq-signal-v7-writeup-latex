\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Context and Motivation}

Secure communication has quietly become part of the infrastructure of everyday life, and the cryptography that makes it possible is now facing a challenge it was never designed for. The arrival of quantum computing threatens to undermine the mathematical foundations on which today's secure messaging rests, and the systems that protect billions of conversations are having to adapt before that threat fully materialises. Signal, one of the most widely used of these systems, has already begun that adaptation, but the choices it has made along the way invite a closer look. This chapter sets out the ground from which that closer look proceeds: what secure messaging requires, why the quantum threat matters, how Signal has responded, and what its response leaves open.

\subsubsection{Secure Messaging in the Modern Era}

Secure messaging has moved from a specialist concern to a baseline expectation of modern digital communication. At the centre of this shift is end-to-end encryption (E2EE), in which messages are encrypted on the sender's device and decrypted only on the recipient's device, such that neither the service provider nor any intermediary on the network can read message content. Billions of daily conversations now depend on E2EE messaging, most of them protected by the Signal Protocol or by derivatives of it deployed in other messaging applications \cite{cohn-gordon-signal}.

The guarantees that users implicitly expect from a secure messenger extend well beyond in-transit confidentiality. Modern secure messaging protocols are expected to provide forward secrecy, so that compromise of a device's current keys does not expose past conversations; post-compromise security, so that compromise does not permanently expose future conversations either; and asynchronous operation, so that two users can establish a secure session without both being online at the same time \cite{cohn-gordon-signal}. The Signal Protocol meets these requirements through a layered design that combines an initial key agreement protocol \cite{x3dh-spec} with a continuous ratcheting mechanism that evolves session keys with every message exchanged \cite{double-ratchet-spec}.

Because secure messaging now underpins personal, professional, journalistic, and political communication, the cryptographic foundations on which these protocols rest carry consequences that reach well beyond their immediate users. A weakness in the cryptography used by a major messenger is not a local defect; it is a systemic risk to a significant fraction of global private communication. This position has made secure messaging one of the first application domains in which the wider cryptographic community has begun to confront a threat that, until recently, belonged to the future.

\subsubsection{The Quantum Threat and Harvest-Now-Decrypt-Later}

The threat in question is the prospect of a sufficiently powerful quantum computer. Peter Shor's 1994 algorithm \cite{shor1994} demonstrated that a quantum computer capable of running it at sufficient scale would solve the integer factorisation problem and the discrete logarithm problem in polynomial time. The public-key cryptosystems that protect the overwhelming majority of internet traffic, including RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, derive their security from the assumed classical intractability of exactly these problems. A cryptographically relevant quantum computer, once built, would therefore render these cryptosystems insecure.

Whether such a machine will be built, and if so when, remains uncertain. Current engineering estimates place the threshold far enough in the future that transitional planning is possible but close enough that it is not optional \cite{mosca2018}. The concrete danger, however, does not wait for the machine itself. Under the harvest-now-decrypt-later (HNDL) threat model, an adversary intercepts and stores encrypted traffic today, with the intention of decrypting it later once a suitable quantum computer becomes available \cite{mosca2018}. Any communication whose confidentiality needs to outlive the gap between now and that future moment is therefore effectively exposed to quantum attack already, at the time it is transmitted, regardless of the protocol's strength against present-day adversaries.

Secure messaging is particularly exposed to this model. Long-running conversations carry personal, professional, and political information whose sensitivity may persist for decades, and the asynchronous, server-mediated architecture of messaging systems such as Signal means that ciphertext and long-term key material pass through and are sometimes retained by infrastructure outside the user's control. It is this exposure, rather than the arrival of quantum computers themselves, that has driven the migration of secure messaging toward post-quantum cryptography.

\subsubsection{Signal's Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography}

Signal's response to the harvest-now-decrypt-later threat began in 2023 with the replacement of its X3DH (Extended Triple Diffie-Hellman) initial key agreement protocol by a new protocol, PQXDH (Post-Quantum Extended Triple Diffie-Hellman) \cite{pqxdh-spec, kret-pq-blog}. PQXDH retains the structure of X3DH and adds a post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism, CRYSTALS-Kyber (since standardised as ML-KEM), alongside the existing elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman operations. The two shared secrets, one from the elliptic-curve component and one from the post-quantum component, are combined through a key derivation function, so that an attacker must break both the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem and the underlying problem of the post-quantum KEM in order to recover the session key. This construction is described by Signal as hybrid, and is motivated on the grounds that the classical component remains a well-understood baseline while the post-quantum component defends against the HNDL threat \cite{kret-pq-blog}.

PQXDH was the first deployment step, not the last. Signal has since introduced further post-quantum work in the continuous ratcheting layer, through the Sparse Post-Quantum Ratchet (SPQR) design, which extends post-quantum protection from the initial key agreement into the ongoing session and addresses post-compromise security in a quantum-relevant threat model \cite{spqr-blog}. Combined with the existing Double Ratchet, SPQR forms what Signal now calls the Triple Ratchet \cite{double-ratchet-spec, spqr-blog}. The overall trajectory, as publicly set out, is a staged migration of Signal's cryptographic core to post-quantum equivalents, with PQXDH as its earliest and most widely deployed component.

Signal's work therefore represents one of the first large-scale post-quantum deployments in a consumer-facing secure messaging system, and it has been subject to formal security analysis by independent researchers who have examined the structure of PQXDH and verified its core security properties \cite{bhargavan-pqxdh}. What this body of work has not addressed, however, is whether the specific post-quantum primitives chosen by Signal represent the best available choice, or even a well-characterised choice, within the wider post-quantum landscape. The public record contains strong verification that PQXDH, as constructed, is secure under its own assumptions, but it contains no comparative evaluation of whether alternative primitives would yield better performance, lower bandwidth, lower storage cost, or greater diversity of cryptographic assumption. That question is the starting point for the remainder of this dissertation.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Signal's PQXDH protocol combines X25519 alongside ML-KEM-1024 in a hybrid construction \cite{pqxdh-spec}. Signal's published rationale motivates this hybrid approach as a conservative safety measure against crypto-analytic progress on either primitive and describes ML-KEM as resting on solid foundations \cite{pqxdh-spec, kret-pq-blog}. What Signal has not published, however, is any comparative evaluation of alternative post-quantum chems or signature schemes in its deployment context, nor a quantitative analysis of how its specific constraints (namely asynchronous messaging, mobile-first clients, and server-side pre-key bundle storage at scale) bear on this choice.

This is a completely different assessment made from the equivalent question in TLS 1.3, where the IETF TLS working group has produced public drafts, extensive performance studies, and code points for multiple primitive combinations in both hybrid and pure post-quantum forms \cite{tls13-pq-a, tls13-pq-b, tls13-pq-c}. Moreover, the substitution of one post-quantum primitive for another in PQXDH is not a trivial library swap. The formal analysis by Bhargavan et al. \cite{bhargavan-pqxdh} identifies properties that PQXDH relies on, in particular the binding of the shared secret to the KEM public key, that some alternative KEMs such as HQC and BIKE do not satisfy. A comparative evaluation therefore demands that each candidate be examined against the protocol's requirements, rather than dropped in as a notional replacement.

In the absence of such a study, there is no public empirical basis on which to assess whether ML-KEM represents an optimal, Pareto-optimal, or merely sufficient choice for Signal's constraints, nor on which to weigh the cost of the hybrid construction against its conservative benefit.

This dissertation addresses that gap. It implements the PQXDH protocol with alternative post-quantum primitive combinations and benchmarks their handshake latency, bandwidth consumption, and storage overhead against the PQXDH instantiation currently deployed by Signal. The alternative instantiations built for this work are fully post-quantum, with the X25519 component replaced rather than augmented; the justification for this choice, together with the criteria used to select candidate primitives, is developed in Chapter 3.

\subsection{Aims and Objectives}

\subsubsection{Project Aims}

The overarching aim of this project is to examine the design space of post-quantum primitive selection for Signal's PQXDH key agreement protocol \cite{pqxdh-spec}, both through structured analysis of the cryptographic literature and through empirical measurement of a working implementation, and to situate Signal's currently-deployed primitive choices within that design space on the basis of comparative evidence rather than inference.

To serve this overarching aim, the project sets out the following specific aims.

The first specific aim is to synthesise the literature on post-quantum cryptographic primitives, covering key encapsulation mechanisms, digital signature schemes, and the families of underlying mathematical assumptions on which those primitives rest \cite{pqc-survey-a, pqc-survey-b}. The synthesis takes in the primitives selected and progressed by the NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography standardisation process, which functions as the dominant organising reference point in the field, but extends beyond it to include schemes that were eliminated from that process yet remain analytically relevant, as well as schemes developed outside it. This broader scope is deliberate: one of the threads this project examines is the extent to which Signal's implicit reliance on NIST-standardised primitives, discussed in Chapter 2, forecloses on design options whose merits deserve evaluation on their own terms.

The second specific aim is to examine how the question of post-quantum migration has been treated in other widely-deployed cryptographic protocols, including TLS 1.3 \cite{tls13-pq-a, tls13-pq-b, tls13-pq-c} SSH, the Messaging Layer Security protocol, and Apple's iMessage PQ3 deployment, and to extract from that treatment the design decisions, trade-offs, and lessons that those communities have documented in public.

The third specific aim is to analyse the published rationale underlying Signal's PQXDH design \cite{pqxdh-spec, kret-pq-blog}, to identify the specific questions that rationale does not publicly address, and to articulate the corresponding research gap in terms concrete enough to support a programme of comparative evaluation.

The fourth specific aim is to develop a structured framework for evaluating post-quantum primitives as candidate replacements for those currently used in PQXDH, drawing on both the synthesised literature and the formal properties that PQXDH is known to require \cite{bhargavan-pqxdh}.

The fifth specific aim is to produce a working implementation of PQXDH instantiated with alternative post-quantum primitive combinations, and to design a benchmarking methodology appropriate to the deployment context in which Signal operates.

The sixth specific aim is to analyse the resulting comparative evidence and use it to position Signal's currently-deployed primitive choices within the observed design space, drawing implications both for PQXDH and for the wider class of post-quantum secure messaging protocol designs of which PQXDH is an early and widely-studied exemplar.

\subsubsection{Research Questions}

The work is organised around three research questions.

\textbf{RQ1} -- Which post-quantum primitive combinations constitute plausible candidates for instantiating PQXDH, and what criteria should govern their selection given Signal's deployment context?

Answering this question requires a survey of the available post-quantum key encapsulation mechanisms and signature schemes; a mapping of PQXDH's protocol-level requirements, including the binding and contributory properties identified by Bhargavan et al. \cite{bhargavan-pqxdh}, onto the properties each candidate provides; and the construction of a selection framework that accounts for security level, mathematical assumption diversity, performance, bandwidth and storage cost, standardisation status, and library maturity. This question is addressed in Chapters 2 and 3.

\textbf{RQ2} -- How do the selected post-quantum primitive combinations compare, on Signal-relevant metrics, both to each other and to the PQXDH instantiation currently deployed by Signal \cite{pqxdh-spec}?

Answering this question requires a working implementation of each selected combination; a benchmarking methodology that captures handshake latency, per-primitive operation cost, pre-key bundle size, bandwidth per session establishment, and server-side storage overhead; and a set of experimental results with appropriate statistical treatment and reporting. This question is addressed in Chapters 4 and 5.

\textbf{RQ3} -- What does the comparative evidence reveal about he design space of PQXDH, and what implications does it carry for the primitive choices made in the deployed protocol and for the wider class of post-quantum secure messaging protocols?

Answering this question requires interpretation of the benchmark data in the context of Signal's real deployment constraints; identification of whether the results exhibit a dominant choice, Pareto-optimal trade-offs, or regime-dependent behaviour; and a positioning of Signal's current instantiation within the observed design space. This question is addressed in Chapters 5 and 7.

\subsubsection{Contributions}

This dissertation makes five contributions.

C1. A structured literature review of post-quantum cryptographic primitives and of how widely deployed cryptographic protocols, including TLS 1.3, SSH, MLS, and iMessage PQ3, have approached post-quantum migration, synthesising the design patterns, trade-offs, and lessons that those communities have documented and mapping them onto Signal's context.

C2. An analysis of Signal's published design rationale for PQXDH \cite{pqxdh-spec, kret-pq-blog} and a corresponding selection framework for evaluating alternative post-quantum primitives as candidate replacements, articulating the protocol-level requirements that PQXDH imposes, including those identified by the formal analysis of Bhargavan et al. \cite{bhargavan-pqxdh}, together with the deployment-specific criteria against which each candidate must be judged.

C3. A working implementation of the PQXDH protocol instantiated with alternative fully post-quantum primitive combinations, constructed by integrating published post-quantum cryptographic libraries with the structure of the PQXDH specification \cite{pqxdh-spec}.

C4. A comparative benchmark of the implemented instantiations against the PQXDH instantiation currently deployed by Signal, produced under a documented and reproducible experimental methodology, and reporting handshake latency, per-primitive operation cost, pre-key bundle size, bandwidth per session establishment, and server-side storage overhead.

C5. An analysis of the resulting comparative design space that positions Signal's currently-deployed primitive choices within the observed performance and cost landscape, identifies the conditions under which alternative choices would be preferable, and draws implications for the wider class of post-quantum secure messaging protocols.

Contributions C1 and C2 primarily address RQ1, contributions C3 and C4 primarily address RQ2, and contribution C5 primarily addresses RQ3.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}

\subsubsection{Scope}

The scope of this project is defined by four deliberate boundaries that distinguish what the project promises to deliver from what it does not.

First, the implementation artefact addresses only the PQXDH key agreement protocol \cite{pqxdh-spec}. The Double Ratchet protocol, the Sender Keys protocol for group messaging, the Safety Numbers protocol for key verification, and the encrypted voice and video call protocol all fall outside the scope of the artefact, and no modifications to them are proposed or implemented. Signal's more recent Triple Ratchet and Sparse Post-Quantum Ratchet (SPQR) designs, which address post-compromise security in the continuous ratchet rather than in the initial handshake, are likewise outside the artefact's scope. The literature review in Chapter 2 does, however, discuss these other protocols and designs, both to situate PQXDH within Signal's broader post-quantum roadmap and to extract lessons from how the wider cryptographic community has approached post-quantum migration in other deployed protocols.

Second, the artefact substitutes post-quantum primitives within the structure of the published PQXDH specification. The resulting variants are recognisably PQXDH; what changes between variants is the choice of post-quantum KEM used to generate the post-quantum shared secret, the signature scheme used to sign pre-keys, and whether the classical X25519 component is retained or replaced. The project does not propose a new key agreement protocol, does not modify PQXDH's message flow, and does not alter the handoff to the Double Ratchet.

Third, the implementation is built to support benchmarking and comparative evaluation rather than production deployment. Its purpose is to produce measurements of handshake latency, per-primitive operation cost, pre-key bundle size, bandwidth per session establishment, and server-side storage overhead for each variant under study. Interoperability with Signal's production client and server infrastructure is neither attempted nor claimed.

Fourth, the comparative evaluation is empirical and addresses performance, bandwidth, and storage. The project does not produce a new formal symbolic verification of any modified PQXDH variant. Security reasoning in the project is inherited: the protocol structure's security properties rely on the existing formal analyses of PQXDH \cite{pqxdh-spec, bhargavan-pqxdh}, and the security properties of each substituted primitive rely on that primitive's own published security arguments. The informal security argument developed in Chapters 3 and 5 establishes that each substituted primitive satisfies the protocol-level requirements that PQXDH relies on, and therefore that the existing security argument for the protocol structure continues to apply to each variant. Reproducing the symbolic verification of the modified protocol in a tool such as ProVerif or CryptoVerif is identified as future work.

\subsubsection{Limitations}

Within the scope defined above, the project carries three limitations that readers should weight when interpreting the results.

First, benchmarks are conducted on two desktop-class platforms: an x86-64 system with a Zen 5 processor and an ARM64 system based on Apple's M1. Benchmarking across both architectures captures one axis of hardware variation directly, because several post-quantum primitives have architecture-specific optimised implementations whose relative performance can differ between x86-64 and ARM64. However, the benchmarks do not cover mobile or embedded platforms, and the performance characteristics observed on desktop-class hardware do not necessarily transfer to the constrained devices that make up a large share of Signal's real user base. Mobile benchmarking is a potential extension to the work but is not promised as part of the contribution.

Second, the implementation depends on external post-quantum cryptographic libraries, including reference implementations and optimised implementations where available. Performance results therefore partly reflect the maturity and optimisation level of the underlying libraries rather than the intrinsic cost of the primitives they implement. A primitive whose reference implementation is heavily optimised may appear favourable against one whose implementation is less mature, independently of the underlying algorithm. Chapter 4 documents the library versions and implementation variants used so that the reader can assess the extent to which this limitation affects the comparative conclusions.

Third, the selection of candidate post-quantum primitive combinations for implementation is guided by the literature review in Chapter 2 and the selection framework developed in Chapter 3. This selection is theoretically motivated rather than exhaustive: the goal is to identify the primitives that the literature and the framework together suggest are the most promising candidates for improvement on Signal's current choice, and to benchmark those against Signal's deployed PQXDH. The comparative conclusions therefore rest on the soundness of the theoretical selection. Primitives that are not implemented are discussed in Chapter 2 and may be assessed on paper, but they do not contribute to the measured comparison. This is a deliberate methodological choice, not a shortfall: exhaustive benchmarking of every candidate in the post-quantum landscape would be neither feasible at MSc scale nor particularly useful, given that the research question is about whether better alternatives to Signal's current choice exist, not about producing a complete performance atlas of the post-quantum field.

\subsection{Structure}

The remainder of this dissertation is organised as follows.

Chapter 2 presents the background and literature review. It introduces the fundamentals of secure messaging and the Signal Protocol, describes the X3DH key agreement and the classical cryptographic primitives on which Signal has historically relied, sets out the quantum threat and the cryptanalytic results that motivate post-quantum migration, and surveys the landscape of post-quantum primitives and the NIST standardisation process through which many of them have been selected. The chapter then turns to PQXDH itself and to the published formal analyses of the protocol, surveys the candidate post-quantum KEMs and signature schemes that could plausibly replace or augment Signal's current primitive choices, and examines how other deployed protocols, including TLS 1.3, SSH, MLS, and iMessage PQ3, have approached post-quantum migration. Chapter 2 concludes by reflecting critically on Signal's design choices and articulating the specific research gap that the remainder of the dissertation addresses.

Chapter 3 develops the objectives, requirements, and design of the modified PQXDH variants that this project implements. It restates the project's objectives in the form of concrete functional, security, and non-functional requirements, defines the threat model under which the modified variants are to be evaluated, and develops a structured framework for evaluating candidate post-quantum primitives against the protocol-level requirements that PQXDH imposes. The chapter then applies that framework to select the primitive combinations taken forward into implementation, justifies the decision to build fully post-quantum variants, and presents the high-level design of the modified protocol alongside a theoretical comparison to the PQXDH instantiation currently deployed by Signal.

Chapter 4 describes the methodology and implementation. It documents the choice of LibSignal version and the programming and tooling environment, the selection of post-quantum cryptographic libraries, and the strategy used to integrate the substituted primitives with the structure of the PQXDH protocol. The chapter then describes the implementation of each protocol stage in detail, the testing and validation procedures applied to the resulting code, and the benchmarking methodology under which the implemented variants are measured.

Chapter 5 presents the results, analysis, and evaluation. It reports the benchmarks produced under the methodology of Chapter 4, compares each variant against the PQXDH instantiation currently deployed by Signal on the metrics identified in the research questions, and analyses the resulting performance, bandwidth, and storage trade-offs. The chapter then evaluates the modified variants against the security requirements set out in Chapter 3, discusses unexpected findings, identifies threats to validity, and draws out recommendations for Signal and for similar post-quantum secure messaging protocols.

Chapter 6 addresses the legal, social, ethical, and professional issues raised by the project, covering export controls on cryptographic software, open-source licence compliance, the social debate around secure communication and lawful access, the dual-use nature of cryptographic research, responsible disclosure, and professional standards under the BCS Code of Conduct.

Chapter 7 concludes the dissertation. It summarises the work, revisits each research question in light of the evidence produced, restates the contributions of the project, discusses the limitations of the work as actually delivered, and outlines directions for future work, including the extension of the approach to the Double Ratchet, the formal verification of the modified protocol, and further exploration of cryptographically diverse primitive families.
