\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This dissertation set out to determine whether Signal's deployed PQXDH could be made fully post-quantum within the handshake, at what cost, and with what implications for the wider class of post-quantum secure messaging protocols. It approached the question by implementation and measurement: two fully post-quantum variants of the PQXDH handshake were built inside a fork of Signal's own library, one pairing ML-KEM-1024 with ML-DSA-87 and one substituting HQC-256 for the KEM, and both were benchmarked against the pinned pre-standardisation baseline on the metrics that matter to Signal's deployment, namely handshake latency, message and bundle size, and the derived server-side storage. The work is deliberately scoped to the PQXDH handshake and its transcript authentication. The Double Ratchet continuation, the encrypted payload, and the remainder of the session retain the classical X25519 exchange and are out of scope.

\textbf{RQ1} asked which post-quantum primitive combinations are plausible candidates for instantiating PQXDH, and what criteria should govern their selection. Chapter~\ref{sec:lit} surveyed the candidate primitives on Signal-relevant metrics and Chapter~\ref{sec:design} developed a selection framework from the project's functional, security, and non-functional requirements. The framework narrowed the field to a lattice KEM with a strong standardisation position and a small ciphertext, ML-KEM-1024, and a code-based KEM offering cryptographic diversity at a much larger ciphertext, HQC-256, each paired with ML-DSA-87 as the signature primitive on the grounds of its standardisation and its balance of key and signature sizes.

\textbf{RQ2} asked how the selected combinations compare, to each other and to the baseline. Chapter~\ref{sec:results} answers this and is the empirical core of the project: the ML-KEM-1024 variant is inexpensive, costing on the order of one and a half times the baseline end to end with a responder span that matches or betters it and a message roughly six times larger, while the HQC-256 variant is expensive on both axes, its handshake between ten and sixty times the lattice variant and its initial message more than twenty times the baseline. Both orderings are stable in the methodology's sense across two machines and architectures.

\textbf{RQ3} asked what the comparative evidence reveals about the design space and what it implies for Signal and for post-quantum secure messaging more broadly. The evidence reveals that within the handshake the choice of post-quantum KEM is not a performance dilemma but a size dilemma. The compute cost of even the slower candidate remains at interactive timescale and is paid once per session; the size cost, by contrast, is dominated by the KEM ciphertext, is paid on every fresh session, and does not amortise. For the deployed protocol this supports the choice of a lattice KEM: ML-KEM-1024 imposes a modest and largely one-time overhead, whereas a code-based KEM's kilobyte-scale ciphertext would recur in every initial message and every stored bundle. For the wider class of protocols the finding generalises beyond the handshake. The handshake is the tractable part to make post-quantum; the operation that resists a low-cost post-quantum treatment is not the one-time key agreement but the continuous ratchet and the per-message overhead that follow it, which remain classical in this work and in the deployed protocol alike. The bottleneck for a fully post-quantum session is therefore not the handshake primitives measured here but everything after the handshake.

The project contributes a selection framework grounded in the binding requirement rather than in performance alone and two working fully post-quantum PQXDH handshake implementations inside Signal's own library, released and independently reproducible. It also provides a two-machine benchmark of those implementations against a pinned baseline with an explicit stability criterion and a design-space analysis that locates the true cost of the post-quantum transition in message size rather than in latency. The limitations are recorded in the requirement-to-evidence matrix of Section~\ref{subsec:verification-outcomes}: signature-mediated rather than KDF-bound ciphertext binding, absent constructor-level suite enforcement, no established binding proof for the pinned HQC revision, and performance figures that characterise pre-standardisation implementations on desktop hardware rather than the algorithms in general.

Future work follows from these limitations. The most consequential direction is the extension of the post-quantum treatment beyond the handshake to the Double Ratchet, which the results identify as the operation that governs whether a session can be fully post-quantum rather than merely post-quantum at establishment. Beyond that, the modified handshake warrants formal symbolic analysis before any security claim stronger than the functional one made here, the ciphertext binding and suite enforcement gaps warrant the constructor-level fixes the matrix names, and the comparison would be strengthened by benchmarking on mobile and embedded hardware and by re-measuring against standardised implementations as they become available. The project shows that a fully post-quantum PQXDH handshake is both achievable and cheap when built on a lattice KEM, and that the harder problem, and the more interesting one, lies in the session that follows it.
